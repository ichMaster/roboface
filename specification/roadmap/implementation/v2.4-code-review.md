## 1. 🔴 HIGH — proximity is wired to a bus with nothing on it

**Where:** `firmware/src/app/sensors.h` — `M5.Ex_I2C.begin()` and `M5.Ex_I2C.readRegister(...)`.

**Failure scenario.** M5Unified exposes two buses: `In_I2C` for the devices inside the CoreS3 — the
AXP2101, the touch controller, the IMU, **and the LTR-553** — and `Ex_I2C` for Port A, the external
connector. This code reads the proximity register from `Ex_I2C`, where nothing is connected.

So:

1. `readRegister` fails on every call and `read()` returns 0.
2. 0 is below every threshold, so `ProximityDetector` sits in "nothing there" forever.
3. **No approach is ever detected.** The face never wakes to a hand, the gaze reflex never fires,
   and the `proximity` half of `event{}` never leaves the device.

**And the diagnostic lies about it.** `begin()` returns whatever `Ex_I2C.begin()` returns, which
succeeds regardless of what is on the bus — so the "not present" line never prints and the boot log
says everything is fine. A feature that silently does nothing, on a board that reports itself
healthy, is the worst shape a hardware defect can take: nobody would look here, because nothing
points here.

**Why the tests did not catch it.** They could not: every one of them feeds `ProximityDetector`
directly with counts. The pure logic is correct and thoroughly tested; the defect is entirely in the
four lines of glue that nothing on a laptop can exercise. That is the honest limit of the pure/glue
split, and this finding is what it looks like when the limit is reached.

**Proposed fix.** Read from `In_I2C`, and make `begin()` **prove the sensor is there** rather than
that a bus exists: the LTR-553 answers `0x92` from its part-ID register at `0x86`. A `begin()` that
cannot tell an absent sensor from a present one is not a check.

**Regression test.** None is possible on the host, and saying so is better than inventing one. The
part-ID check *is* the runtime test, and the manual document carries the on-device confirmation.

## 2. 🟠 MEDIUM — the mute gesture also tickles

**Where:** `firmware/src/main.cpp` — the two-finger block and the `TouchGestures` block run over the
same panel state in the same loop.

**Failure scenario.**

1. A person taps with two fingers to mute the microphone.
2. The two-finger block sees the count reach 2 and toggles listening. Correct.
3. `M5.Touch.getDetail()` reports the **first** touch, which is pressed and then released — so
   `TouchGestures` classifies a perfectly ordinary tap.
4. The face tickles, affection accumulates, and `event{touch, tap}` goes to the server, which may
   answer with a `joy` frame.

The character has just been delighted by being muted.

**Why it matters beyond the oddity.** DEVICE_UI §Input separates **affection** from **control** and
says control gestures are local UI and are deliberately not reported — the character has no business
knowing the person opened a menu or silenced the microphone. This leaks one directly across that
line, and it is the line the whole gesture table is organised around.

**Proposed fix.** A multi-finger touch is not affection. `TouchGestures` is fed only while exactly
one finger is down, and a second finger arriving cancels whatever the first was becoming.

**Regression test.** In pure logic: a gesture stream that reports two fingers produces nothing.

## 3. 🟠 MEDIUM — a reset that exists for a reason nobody calls

**Where:** `firmware/src/pure/touch.h` — `TouchGestures::reset()`, and `main.cpp`, which never
invokes it.

**Failure scenario.** The method's own comment states the case: *"called when the face changes state
under the finger — a run that spanned a reply would report a count nobody performed."* Nothing calls
it, so the case it describes is live:

1. A person taps the face. `count = 1`, a tickle.
2. They speak; a turn runs; the device replies.
3. Within 400 ms of the *first* tap they tap again — which after a turn is unlikely, but a tap right
   before a short reply and one right after is not.
4. `count = 2`: a multi-tap, deeper joy, and `event{touch, multi_tap}` reporting a gesture that was
   never performed as one.

**The broader smell is the dead call.** A method written for a documented situation and never wired
up means either the situation is not real — in which case the method should go — or it is real and
unhandled. Here it is the second, and the comment already says so.

**Proposed fix.** Call it where the device's state changes under the finger: `apply()`, which is the
one place every transition passes through.

**Regression test.** A tap, a reset, a tap — the second reports `count = 1`.

## What's solid

- **Every threshold carries the number it came from.** Free fall at 0.35 g because a real drop
  tumbles; 120 ms because a desk is 70 cm and a fall lasts 380; shake at 1.8 g because a desk knock
  peaks near 1.3. After three phases of thresholds picked by feel and paid for later, this is the
  phase that stopped doing it.
- **The false-positive tests are the ones with the most care in them**, which is the right
  allocation: a device on a desk for thirty seconds, sensor noise, a sleeve passing over, a hand
  hovering at the boundary. Those fire when nobody is watching, and the others fire while someone is
  holding the device and can see why.
- **The refusal in `event{}` is argued next to the coercion in `emotion{}`**, and there is a test
  holding the two side by side. Neither decision is defensible alone.
- **The serial-receive limitation is asserted rather than wished away.** The test says what actually
  happens, says why it is acceptable, and names the phase that will change it.

# Fixes applied

## #1 — proximity on the internal bus, and a `begin()` that checks

**The change.** `In_I2C` rather than `Ex_I2C`, and `begin()` reads the LTR-553's part-ID register
and compares it to `0x92`. Checking that a bus exists and checking that a sensor answers are
different questions, and only the second one is worth asking.

**No regression test, and that is the finding rather than an omission.** Every proximity test feeds
`ProximityDetector` counts directly, which is right — the decision belongs in pure logic and is
thoroughly covered there. The defect was in four lines of glue that nothing on a host can exercise,
and inventing a test that mocked an I2C bus would prove that the mock was wired correctly.

**Architecture impact.** None to a contract, but worth recording as a limit: **the pure/glue split
means the glue is checked by compiling and by hand, and this is what happens when the glue is
wrong.** The part-ID check is the closest thing to a runtime test available, and it converts a
silent nothing into a boot-log line.

## #2 — control is not affection

**The change.** `TouchSample` carries the finger count, and more than one cancels the gesture in
progress. Cancelling rather than ignoring is the part that matters: a gesture half-formed when the
second finger arrived must not complete after it leaves.

**The regression test.** Three cases — two fingers produce nothing, a second finger cancels a
gesture already begun, and one finger is still affection. The last is there because the obvious
wrong fix is one that quietly ignores everything.

## #3 — the reset that nothing called

**The change.** `apply()` calls `gestures.reset()`, which is the one place every device state
transition passes through.

**Architecture impact.** None. Worth a note only for the smell that found it: a method written for a
documented situation and never wired up means either the situation is not real, in which case the
method should go, or it is real and unhandled. It was the second, and the method's own comment said
so.

## Suggested next actions

1. `release-version 2.4.0`.
2. **The manual document, and for this phase it carries more weight than for any before it.** Five
   of the six issues end in a physical action; the DoD is a claim about 100 ms; and finding #1 is a
   defect only a hand in front of the sensor can confirm is gone.
